# Estimation Calibration Note

Status: Active · Date: 2026-08-22 · Author: Prasadmallavalli

The "planned" baseline for this note is real, not reconstructed after the fact: [`brief.md`'s addendum](../../_bmad-output/planning-artifacts/briefs/brief-ASPFullStackBMAD-2026-08-18/addendum.md) laid out an 18-session, ~40-46 hour plan *before* any of Epics 1-5 were built, with explicit per-session hour estimates (e.g., "Session 15 (2h) — Roadmap + Estimation"). "Actual" comes from git commit timestamps — the only real, timestamped record of when work landed. That pairing is honest but imperfect, and the biggest calibration lesson here is exactly where and why it breaks down.

## The one big miscalibration: the unit of estimation itself was wrong

The addendum's Part A (Sessions 1-10, ~20h) planned **human hands-on coding hours** — a person typing through ASP.NET Core fundamentals, EF Core, DI, SOLID, one topic per 2-hour block. Parts B-D (Sessions 11-18, ~20-26h) planned artifact-writing time instead — ADRs, a design doc, a checklist, a postmortem, a roadmap — closer in kind to what actually happened, though still assuming a person drafting solo. What actually happened, for both parts, was BMAD-methodology, AI-agent-driven implementation: each story went through a spec → implementation → adversarial review (blind-hunter, often 10-15 findings) → patch → commit cycle, with the human's actual time going into spec approval and finding triage, not hands-on typing or solo drafting. Comparing "planned hours" to "actual hours" for this kind of work isn't apples-to-apples — the thing being estimated (a human learning-and-typing or solo-drafting session) isn't the thing that actually happened (an agent implementing against an approved contract, adversarially reviewed, then patched). Any hour-for-hour comparison below is stated with that caveat, not as a precise metric.

A second, more literal way the unit of estimation was wrong: the addendum's Session 15 explicitly asked for "a lightweight estimation pass on the sessions themselves in hindsight" — a session-by-session accounting. The actual build has no sessions to account for in that sense; it has stories and commits. This note substitutes the closest real equivalent (commit-timestamped spans, below) rather than forcing a session-shaped answer onto a story-shaped delivery record.

## Where actual diverged from planned, and why — four real examples

(Sessions 16-18 — hiring questions, STAR extraction, extended capstone — aren't included below: as of this note, they map to work not yet built, so there's no actual to compare against a plan. The four examples below are every session-group with real, timestamped delivery data.)

**1. Sessions 1-7 (~14h planned) are uncomparable, and that itself is the finding.** The addendum planned seven distinct 2-hour sessions (Web API fundamentals, EF Core, DI, SOLID, Design Patterns, DTOs/Validation, JWT+Identity) as separately-timed learning blocks. In actual delivery, all of that — plus Stories 2.1 and 2.2 — shipped as a single `git init` "Initial commit" (`f1bc57f`), because version control wasn't started until after that work already existed. There is no internal timestamp to compare against the plan's seven session boundaries; the evidence trail simply doesn't exist for 14 of the addendum's planned ~40-46 total hours — roughly 30-35% of the plan, the exact share depending on which end of that range is used. **Why it diverged:** the plan assumed version control would exist from Session 1, but it wasn't started until partway through. **Process lesson:** `git init` before the first session, not after — losing the timestamp trail for over a third of the planned work is a real, avoidable estimation-tracking gap, not a scope problem.

**2. The login-form story didn't exist in the plan at all — a scope miss, not a time miss.** The addendum's React/CRUD sessions (Session 8: "enough React to consume the API"; Session 9: "Full CRUD Integration") never mention building a login form — Session 7's JWT/Identity work is backend-only. Partway through Story 3.3 (Create Product), the build hit a real blocking dependency: there was no way to log in, so there was no way to demonstrate an authenticated create flow. This forced an unplanned story split, recorded at the time in [`deferred-work.md`](../../_bmad-output/implementation-artifacts/deferred-work.md) ("Split from: Story 3.3 intent" section): *"User asked to fold 'add a minimal login form' into the Story 3.3 intent so it's demoable end-to-end, but a login form and Create Product are two independently shippable deliverables... User chose to split: the login form ships first as its own spec (`spec-epic-3-prereq-login-form.md`), Create Product follows as its own spec afterward."* **Why it diverged:** the plan estimated the *backend* auth session but never accounted for the *frontend* form that backend needed before anything else in the CRUD flow could be demoed — a real dependency the session-level plan didn't surface until the build actually hit it. **Process lesson:** a plan built session-by-session-per-topic can miss cross-cutting dependencies that only become visible once integration is attempted, not before.

**3. Session 15's combined "Roadmap + Estimation" (2h planned) became two separate, independently-reviewed stories.** The addendum bundles the roadmap and this estimation note into one 2-hour session. In actual delivery (BMAD's epics/stories structure), they became Story 5.2 and Story 5.3 — two separate specs, two separate blind-hunter review passes (5.2's review alone surfaced 13 findings — see [`spec-5-2-post-mvp-roadmap.md`](../../_bmad-output/implementation-artifacts/spec-5-2-post-mvp-roadmap.md)'s Suggested Review Order for the patched list — each fact-checked against `deferred-work.md`, the design doc, and the postmortem before being patched). **Why it diverged:** the addendum's session-level granularity assumed both artifacts were small enough to combine; the actual review discipline this project applies to every artifact (adversarial review + fact-checking against real sources, not just writing and moving on) doesn't compress well into a shared session, because each artifact needs its own independent review pass to be credible. **This isn't a miss so much as a calibration signal:** the real cost of *this* project's artifacts isn't drafting them — it's the review-and-correction cycle that makes them defensible under interview follow-up questions, and that cost scales per-artifact, not per-session.

**4. Session 11's "ADR Consolidation + Design Doc" (2h planned) also split into two independently-reviewed stories, and one of them exposes a measurement problem, not just a planning one.** Session 11 planned ADR writing and the scaling design doc as one 2-hour block; actual delivery split them into Story 4.1 (6 ADRs) and Story 4.2 (the design doc), each with its own spec and blind-hunter pass — the same "planned-combined, actual-split" pattern as example 3. But the commit gap between Story 5.1 (the postmortem, mapped to Session 14, planned 2h) and the preceding Epic 4 retro commit is only ~9 minutes (`1a4c378` at 09:53 → `b9e09d2` at 10:02) — nowhere near enough elapsed time to have drafted and reviewed a postmortem from scratch. **Why it diverged:** this isn't evidence the postmortem took 9 minutes; it's evidence that commit timestamps are the wrong instrument for measuring AI-assisted work at all, since a single authoring turn (context compilation, drafting, a dispatched review subagent, patching 11 findings) can produce a commit far faster than the underlying work took, with nothing in the git log distinguishing that from a trivial one-line change. **Process lesson:** for this delivery model, "time planned" and "time elapsed" are measuring different things, and neither is a substitute for the other — if this book of estimation data mattered for real planning, the unit to track would be review cycles and findings-per-artifact, not hours.

The last data row is the clearest illustration of why this table is a proxy, not a metric: 52 minutes of commit-to-commit elapsed time covered Story 5.1's postmortem (see example 4 above) plus Story 5.2's fully-reviewed roadmap (13 findings — see [`spec-5-2-post-mvp-roadmap.md`](../../_bmad-output/implementation-artifacts/spec-5-2-post-mvp-roadmap.md)) — and the row before it, the ~35-hour multi-day gap, represents the retrospective covering 15 cross-document findings (see [`epic-4-retro-2026-08-22.md`](../../_bmad-output/implementation-artifacts/epic-4-retro-2026-08-22.md)) that happened entirely *after* that gap, not during it. Commit cadence measures landing frequency, not effort — most of the actual work in an AI-assisted session happens inside a single commit's authoring turn, invisible to a timestamp diff. That gap between what's measurable and what's real is the estimation-calibration lesson this table actually teaches.

## Takeaway, if this data had to inform a real future plan

Every comparable example above skews the same direction — more distinct review cycles than the addendum's session-per-topic model assumed, not fewer. That's not evidence of a balanced "sometimes over, sometimes under" record; it's a directional signal that this project's actual cost driver is the adversarial-review-and-patch cycle per artifact, which the original plan never budgeted for as its own line item. A future estimate for this kind of work should budget review cycles per artifact, not hours per topic — and should track findings-per-review as the leading indicator, since that number (10-15 per artifact, consistently) turned out to be far more stable across this build than any hour estimate was.
